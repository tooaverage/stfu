# Before and after

Pairs from different kinds of products, one per rule. Copy the kind of change, not the words or the layout.

## Landing page hero

Before: "Whether you're a solo freelancer or a growing studio, our powerful platform helps you seamlessly manage invoices, track payments and get paid faster. It's that simple."

After: "Send invoices and track who's paid."

Why: it says what the product does in 6 words. The filler opener, hype words and summary closer go, and so does "get paid faster", which has no number behind it.

## Answer first

Before: "The short answer is yes, you can restore a deleted file. Deleted files stay in your trash for 30 days, and during that time you can bring them back with all of their history. The longer answer depends on your plan, because Business plans keep deleted files for a full year instead of 30 days."

After: "Yes. Deleted files stay in your trash for 30 days, with their history, and you can restore them from there. On Business plans, they stay for a year."

Why: 55 words became 28, and every fact stayed. "The short answer is" and "The longer answer depends on" were setups.

## Guide intro

Before: "Two-factor sign-in is one of the best ways to protect your account, and it's surprisingly easy to set up once you know where to look. This guide walks you through every step, from choosing a method to saving your backup codes."

After: "Turn on two-factor sign-in with an authenticator app or a text message. Steps for each are below, plus how to save your backup codes."

Why: the reader came to turn it on, so that comes first. "This guide walks you through" narrates the page, and "surprisingly easy" is a promise, not a fact.

## Section intro

Heading: "3 ways to export your data"

Before: "There are three ways to export your data. All of them include the same files and the same history, so pick whichever feels most comfortable."

After: "All 3 include the same files and history."

Why: the heading already says there are three ways. The one useful fact stays.

## Warning

Before: "Please note that deleting a workspace can't be undone. Once it's deleted, every project inside it is deleted too, and you won't be able to get them back. Make sure you export anything you want to keep before you continue."

After: "Deleting a workspace deletes its projects for good. Export anything you want to keep first."

Why: three sentences said "this is permanent" three ways. The caveat is shorter but still there.

## Error message

Before: "An unexpected error has occurred while attempting to process your request. Please try again later."

After: "That file's too big. The limit is 25 MB. Try a smaller file, or compress this one."

Why: it says what went wrong and how to fix it, with the real number.

## Settings page

Before: every section heading has a line under it. "Profile: You, in this workspace." "Members: Everyone here and the machines they've enrolled." "Delete workspace: Ask the team to permanently remove this workspace and its data."

After: "Profile" and "Members" stand alone. "Delete workspace" keeps one line: "Removes this workspace and its data for good."

Why: a teammate's feedback was "why do we need all these settings instructions". The headings already say what each section is. Only the delete line warns about something the reader needs to know first.

## Empty state

Before: "It looks like you haven't created any projects yet! Get started by clicking the button below to create your very first project."

After: "No projects yet. Add one to track its hours and send invoices from it." with a "New project" button.

Why: the button already says how to start, so the sentence about the button can go. A bare "No projects yet" is short but not friendly: it doesn't say what a project is for or why to make one.

## Hype paragraph

Before: "In today's fast-paced world, our robust platform empowers teams to seamlessly streamline their workflow and unlock their full potential."

After: Ask what the product actually does first. With that fact: "Cut your weekly status meeting. Bramble shows what each person shipped, updated every morning."

Why: the before has no facts in it, only hype words. Shorter hype is still hype, so find the fact.

## Table, not cards

Before: three plan cards, each with a paragraph about what it includes.

After: one table with a column per plan and a row each for price, seats, storage and support.

Why: options compared on the same points read faster as a table, and the page gets shorter without losing a fact.

## Chart, not a list of numbers

Before: "Sign-ups grew from 1,200 in January to 1,450 in February, 1,900 in March and 2,600 in April."

After: a bar chart with each month's number on its bar, and one line: "Sign-ups more than doubled from January to April."

Why: numbers that change over time are quicker to see than to read. The labels on the bars keep every number.

## Map, not a list of places

Before: six store addresses, each with a paragraph about parking and opening hours.

After: a map with a labeled point for each store, then one line per store with its address and hours.

Why: people looking for a store think in places, not addresses. The lines under the map keep every fact as text.

## Folding detail

Before: proxy settings, older operating systems and offline installs sat in the middle of the install steps.

After: the steps stay short, and those details sit under "More install options" that the reader can open.

Why: every reader needs the steps, and only some need the details. They're still on the page, one click away.
